When the bad guys have each other’s backs: The nefarious team of lantana camara and siam weed

By Ananya Naidu · August 2026

The mention of villainous duos may bring up the thought of “Bonnie and Clyde” or “The Wet Bandits” from Home Alone in our heads, and it would be rightfully so, seeing as to how they're intelligent and unpredictable, running from the law, and most importantly, unforgettable.

Though they are highly enticing, they fail to be worthy enough competitors for the extremely wicked pair that is of two terrifyingly invasive plant species. You may be thinking, Plants?

Whatever could they do to outperform the most notorious criminals of all the time? Get ready for a dive into the world of this trespassing twosome that is wreaking havoc in multiple countries by simply existing. They’ve left their homeland and travelled far and wide, and have achieved a lot in the relatively short time they have spent here.

The names of our antagonists are Lantana camara and Chromolaena odorata, better known as the Common Lantana and Siam Weed. Both have a pretty nasty reputation of being unfortunately abundant across our country from the Western Ghats to the Himalayas, as well in 50 other countries of the world.

Lantana camara is an extremely adaptable flowering plant, native to the tropics of the Americas. In the 1800s it was imported to decorate colonial gardens in India, but they did not know that it can inhabit a wide variety of ecosystems; once it has been introduced into a habitat it spreads rapidly. We can say that it surely went on to create a big name for itself in the world of plant crime, as it now ranks among the top ten in the IUCN's "List of the world's 100 worst invasive species". This is the more prominent one of the two, with bright colourful flowers and having been researched more so.

The relatively lesser known half of our equation is Siam Weed or “jack in the bush”. Also brought in India in the 1800s in a botanical garden in Kolkata, It is a rapidly growing perennial flowering shrub, which can behave as a creeper, growing on other vegetation reaching up to 6 m. It can grow in a variety of vegetation compositions, including woodlands , fields, and bone-dry bushveld.

What a formidable team! How do they coordinate their master plan? The key lies in the fact that where one weed slows down in a season, the other takes over. Siam Weed is opportunistic, and it rapidly grows during the wet season, exploding in height and choking out open space almost overnight. As the dry season sets in and Siam Weed dries out, Lantana steps in. Armed with a deep, persistent root system, Lantana stays green and photosynthesizes throughout drought periods in vast regions in our country. This continuous team effort ensures that native vegetation never gets a seasonal break to recover or re-establish. These plants are working together to keep the competition out without having any sort of dialogue between them.

The way they both kill their native neighbours is even more astonishing - by outright chemical warfare. You may have heard of allelopathy in plants before, where they release toxic biochemicals (phenolic compounds and terpenes) through their roots and decaying leaves into the surrounding dirt. The real battle is fought underground. Here, Lantana actively alters soil chemistry to stop native seeds from germinating and its buddy Siam Weed releases phytotoxins that suppress the root growth of neighboring plants. This sort of turns fertile ground into a chemical barrier where native seedlings simply fail to take root. Though it sounds like it's something straight out of a hit techno-thriller, their offences don't end just quite yet.

Physically, they occupy separate structural layers. Yet they are able to create a double punch overgrowth, forming an impenetrable green wall. Siam Weed acts as a vine-like shrub, climbing upward over young trees and low vegetation to block sunlight from above. Lantana forms dense, woody thickets that completely blanket the understory. Adding to this they also become hyper-flammable fuel just waiting to be the cause of large scale forest fires destroying acres and acres of forest land. They are hardly noticed as being out of place by any people, leaving them with full freedom to spread across continents as they please and as fast as they want.

Whew! These two are a serious masterclass in biological invasion. No wonder their control is extremely difficult, seeing as to how they propose a very unique ecological challenge rather than being just two separate annoying plants. Researchers are actually questioning if the effort against these plants is “a battle lost”.

Can our situation be this hopeless? Will these two innocent looking species operating on a global frequency never be defeated? The easiest answer that comes to our mind is “burn it!”, but that method is terrible, for many, many reasons. First off, burning alone will not kill Lantana or Siam weed. Yes! While burning destroys the visible top growth, both of these resilient invasive plants quickly resprout from their root systems. Furthermore, fire can also trigger a massive flush of new seed germination from the soil seed bank. Controlled burns were tested in regions of eastern Australia and South Africa but the area grew back even denser. Secondly, burning over the sheer magnitude of land these weeds are spread pose extreme wildfire risks, severe air pollution and health risks and would destroy the topsoil.

Don't lose hope just yet. Surprisingly, we can once again rely on mother nature herself and look toward natural biocontrol agents to at least slow down the horrifying rate at which lantana and siam weed have spread. Some specific species of pathogens and insects have shown to be damaging the weed significantly in the wild. They are able to lessen the plant’s viability and prevent its further spread by stressing plants and reducing seeding processes. For Lantana, there is a lace bug (amongst other insects) that feeds on the undersides of leaves and deprives the root system of sugars. Siam weed can be controlled by stem galling insects that act as "energy sinks," diverting nutrients away from leaves and flowers. A huge advantage of using these agents is that their populations spread naturally across vast, inaccessible terrain without human labor and they exclusively feed on the target weed, leaving native plants untouched.It is a matter of communities coming together to fund the clearance of prominent areas, government initiatives that take controlled and sustainable incentives into importance, and spreading awareness about the negatives of invasive species in the general public for us to finally catch these “criminals”.
