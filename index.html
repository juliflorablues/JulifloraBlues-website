Our Mission

Not every green is good — and not every invasive is evil.

Juliflora Blues exists to challenge a common notion: that every green is good, and every plant that isn't native is an enemy to be wiped out. The truth sits in the middle. Certain invasive species genuinely crowd out native ecosystems, choke waterways, and strip land of the biodiversity it depends on — and left unchecked, they are a real threat to the ecosystems around them.

But our mission isn't eradication. It's regulation — controlling the scale and pace at which invasive species expand, rather than pretending they can or should simply disappear.

The nuance we build our work around

Almost every invasive species that's considered "bad" also carries something genuinely useful — medicinal, ecological, or cultural. Our research looks for that value deliberately, instead of writing a species off entirely.

We do this by building a scientific curriculum around invasive species — one that's honest about the damage they cause, but doesn't stop there. We research the medicinal and anthropological value that certain invasive species already hold in the communities that live alongside them, and we try to associate real, documented good with plants that are usually only spoken about as threats.

Our Founder

Sartaki Mohanty

Founder

Sartaki Mohanty

Biologist by training. Grew up watching Juliflora take over the landscape long before she had a word for what she was looking at.

"

As a kid, I travelled a lot with my mother across my hometown, and everywhere we went, the same plant seemed to be there — Prosopis Juliflora. I didn't have a name for it then. I just thought it was part of the landscape.

It was only later, when I chose biology as my specialisation, that I understood what I'd actually been looking at for most of my childhood. These plants were invasive. They were doing real damage to the ecosystems they'd spread into — and most people around me had no idea, because a plant that's green and growing doesn't read as a threat.

That's when the idea for Juliflora Blues took shape. A lot of people assume that anything green is automatically good for the environment. Part of my responsibility, as I see it, is to make people aware that isn't true — while resisting the opposite mistake, too. Invasive doesn't have to mean worthless. Something good can come out of understanding these species properly, and I wanted to build an organisation dedicated to finding it.

Our Values

What guides the work

Awareness over assumption

We'd rather help people understand a species than let them assume "green means good" or "invasive means worthless."

Regulate, don't eradicate

Our default is to manage the scale of a species' spread, not to chase full removal.

Evidence before conclusions

Every claim we make about a species' value or harm is something we've researched, not assumed.

Built in the open

Our content, guides, and research are volunteer-led and published for anyone to use.

Glossary

A–Z: terms worth knowing

A working dictionary of environmental science and invasive-species terms, curated by our team and expanded over time.

A
Adaptation
A trait or behavior that helps an organism survive and reproduce in its environment.
Alien species
Another term for a non-native species — one introduced, deliberately or accidentally, outside its natural range.
Anthropogenic
Caused or influenced by human activity, as opposed to natural processes.
B
Biodiversity
The variety of plant, animal, fungal, and microbial life found within a given habitat or ecosystem.
Biological control
Using one organism — often a natural predator or pathogen — to manage the population of another, usually invasive, species.
C
Colonization (ecological)
The process by which a species establishes itself in a new area it didn't previously inhabit.
Competitive exclusion
A principle stating that two species competing for the same limited resource can't stably coexist — one typically displaces the other.
D
Degradation (habitat)
The decline in the quality of a habitat, often reducing its ability to support the species that depend on it.
Displacement (ecological)
When one species is pushed out of its niche or territory by another, often more aggressive, species.
E
Ecosystem
A community of living organisms interacting with each other and their physical environment as a system.
Endemic species
A species native to, and found only in, one specific geographic region.
Eradication
The complete removal or elimination of a species from a given area.
Exotic species
A species living outside its native range, introduced by human activity.
F
Fecundity
The reproductive capacity of an organism, often measured by the number of offspring it can produce.
Fragmentation (habitat)
The breaking up of a large, continuous habitat into smaller, isolated patches.
G
Genetic diversity
The range of genetic variation within a species or population, which affects its ability to adapt to change.
H
Habitat
The natural environment in which a particular species normally lives.
Herbivory
The consumption of plant material by animals.
I
Introduced species
A species moved by human activity, intentionally or not, to a location outside its native range.
Invasive species
A non-native species whose introduction causes, or is likely to cause, ecological or economic harm.
K
Keystone species
A species that has a disproportionately large effect on its ecosystem relative to its abundance.
M
Monoculture
The cultivation of a single species or crop over a wide area, often reducing biodiversity.
N
Native species
A species that occurs naturally in a given region, without human introduction.
Naturalized species
A non-native species that has established a stable, self-sustaining population without necessarily displacing natives.
Niche (ecological)
The specific role a species plays within its ecosystem, including its habitat, resources, and interactions.
O
Overgrazing
Excessive grazing that damages vegetation and reduces a habitat's ability to regenerate.
P
Propagule pressure
The number and frequency of individuals of a species introduced to a new area — a key factor in whether it establishes itself.
R
Range (species)
The full geographic area in which a species is naturally found.
Regulation (ecological)
Managing and controlling the scale of a species' spread and impact, without necessarily eliminating it.
Restoration (ecological)
Efforts to return a degraded habitat or ecosystem to a healthier, more natural state.
S
Succession (ecological)
The gradual process by which the species composition of a habitat changes over time.
T
Threatened species
A species at risk of becoming endangered in the near future.
V
Vector (ecological)
An organism or mechanism that spreads a species, seed, or pathogen from one place to another.
